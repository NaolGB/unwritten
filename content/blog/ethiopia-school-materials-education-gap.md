Imagine preparing for a new school year and discovering that the exercise books, pens and other basic supplies you need have become difficult for your family to afford.

For many students in Ethiopia, this is part of the reality of returning to school. While access to education remains an important national priority, the availability and affordability of basic learning materials continue to present significant challenges for students, families and schools.

A classroom may have teachers, students and a timetable, but learning also depends on having the tools to participate. Textbooks, exercise books and writing materials are not luxuries. They are part of the foundation of everyday education.

So, how significant is the school-material gap in Ethiopia, and what does it mean for the students affected by it?

## 1. When students have to share textbooks

One of the clearest indicators of the challenge is textbook availability.

A November 2025 report by Shega, drawing on findings from the World Bank-supported Global Education Policy Dashboard (GEPD), found that only around half of primary school students in Ethiopia have access to textbooks.

The survey covered 300 representative government primary schools and showed little improvement from the 2021 baseline, when textbook access stood at approximately 49%.

This represents a significant reversal from the progress made during the early 2010s, when large-scale textbook distribution programmes helped Ethiopia approach a one-textbook-per-student ratio for many subjects.

The consequences can be seen inside classrooms. Teachers report students sharing textbooks, sometimes with two or three children working from the same book.

Sharing resources can be an immediate way to manage shortages, but it also means students may have less opportunity to read independently, practise exercises or take learning materials home.

A textbook should be something a student can use to explore a subject, not something they have to wait for another student to finish using.

Source: [Shega – Half of Ethiopia's Primary Students May Lack Textbooks as Gains Unravel (November 2025)](https://www.shega.co/news/half-of-ethiopias-primary-students-may-lack-textbooks-as-gains-unravel).

## 2. The rising cost of going back to school

Having access to education does not mean that the costs associated with attending school are easy to manage.

For families, preparing children for a new academic year often involves purchasing exercise books, pens, uniforms, school bags, shoes and other essentials.

These expenses have become increasingly difficult for many households to absorb.

In September 2026, Capital reported that a 50-page exercise book in Addis Ababa was selling for more than 100 ETB, compared with approximately 70 ETB the previous year.

The same report highlighted the experience of a government employee whose back-to-school shopping list for two children was estimated at around 8,000 ETB, representing more than half of his monthly salary.

These examples illustrate how ordinary school supplies can become a considerable household expense.

Several factors contribute to the rising costs. Ethiopia's July 2024 foreign-exchange reform was followed by a significant depreciation of the birr. Since many stationery products, school bags and uniform materials depend on imports or imported inputs, higher costs have been passed along the supply chain to retailers and consumers.

For families with several school-aged children, these expenses can accumulate quickly.

Some parents have responded by repairing and reusing old uniforms, bags and shoes, while others have reconsidered their children's school arrangements.

The challenge is therefore not simply whether schools are available, but whether families can afford the materials that allow their children to participate fully.

Source: [Capital – A Generation Caught Between Ambition and Survival in the Education Economy (27 September 2026)](https://capitalethiopia.com/2026/09/27/a-generation-caught-between-ambition-and-survival-in-the-education-economy/).

## 3. The challenge goes beyond school supplies

Textbooks and stationery are only part of a much wider education challenge.

Students also need safe and functional learning environments, access to water and sanitation, trained teachers and consistent opportunities to attend school.

The 2025 Global Education Policy Dashboard findings reported by Shega also highlighted significant infrastructure gaps.

The proportion of schools with functioning water and toilet facilities declined from 20% in 2021 to 16% in 2025. The disparity between urban and rural schools was particularly pronounced, with only 9% of rural schools having functioning water and toilet facilities, compared with 34% of urban schools.

Conflict, displacement and climate-related emergencies add further pressure. Schools can be damaged, communities displaced and learning interrupted for extended periods.

For children already facing challenges accessing educational resources, these disruptions can make the journey through school even more difficult.

This is an important distinction: providing school materials can address one barrier to learning, but it cannot independently resolve every challenge within the education system.

## 4. What does the humanitarian response tell us?

The scale of the challenge is also reflected in the work being carried out by humanitarian organisations.

According to UNICEF's Ethiopia Humanitarian Situation Report No. 4, covering July and August 2026, UNICEF and its partners:

- Supported 39,949 children to access education in emergencies during July and August.
- Provided learning materials to 47,123 children during the same period.
- Reached a cumulative total of 179,378 children with learning materials by 31 August 2026.
- Supported a cumulative 202,665 children to access education.

These results were achieved against annual targets of 322,880 children for learning materials and 374,093 children for access to education.

The report also highlighted a substantial funding challenge. UNICEF's 2026 humanitarian education appeal required approximately US$35.2 million, but faced an 84% funding gap as of 31 August 2026.

It is important to note that these figures relate to UNICEF's humanitarian education response, rather than the entire national education budget. Nevertheless, they illustrate the difficulty of meeting the needs of children affected by emergencies and other disruptions.

Even as organisations work to restore access to education and provide learning resources, the needs remain significant.

Source: [UNICEF – Ethiopia Humanitarian Situation Report No. 4, July–August 2026](https://www.unicef.org/media/184996/file/UNICEF-Ethiopia-Humanitarian-Situation-Report-No.4-July%20to%20August%202026.pdf).

## 5. Why practical support matters

Addressing Ethiopia's education challenges requires long-term investment in schools, teachers, infrastructure, learning resources and education systems.

However, large-scale improvements do not eliminate the importance of smaller, community-led initiatives.

Providing students with exercise books, pens and other basic materials is one practical way to respond to an immediate need.

These contributions can help students participate in classroom activities, complete written assignments and have access to essential learning tools.

The effectiveness of such initiatives also depends on how they are organised. Understanding what students actually need, working with local schools and community organisations, allocating resources carefully and following up on distribution can help make contributions more meaningful.

A successful school-material initiative is not just about purchasing supplies. It is about making sure those supplies reach the students who need them.

We started off as a high school project in 2015, and have since expanded our efforts and reach by collaboration with other projects and organizations who share our beliefs.

These initiatives represent a small contribution within a much larger educational landscape. They are not a substitute for sustained investment in public education, but they demonstrate how partnerships and locally organised efforts can help respond to practical needs.

We believe that meaningful progress is built through different forms of support working together: public investment, institutional partnerships, community initiatives and individuals willing to contribute.

## 7. Making learning more accessible, together

The challenge facing students in Ethiopia is bigger than the cost of an exercise book or the availability of a textbook.

It is about ensuring that children have the opportunity and resources to participate in education, regardless of their circumstances.

A student should not have to share a textbook because there are not enough copies. A family should not have to struggle to afford basic stationery. And a child's opportunity to learn should not depend solely on the resources available at home.

There is still considerable work to be done, but every meaningful contribution can be part of a wider effort to make education more accessible.

At Unwritten, we remain committed to supporting initiatives that recognise the importance of learning and create practical opportunities for young people.

Because education is not just about getting children into classrooms. It is about giving them the means, support and opportunity to make the most of their time there.

Every student deserves the opportunity to learn. Every contribution can help move us closer to that goal.
